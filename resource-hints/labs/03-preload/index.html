Lab 03 — preload

preload says "this page needs this resource, now, at this priority" — it moves discovery, not download speed. It is the sharpest hint and the easiest to misuse: every preload competes for bandwidth with everything else on the page, including the thing you were trying to make faster.

Throttle to Fast 4G, hard-reload each page, and compare LCP. Keep the console open — Chrome warns about wasted and duplicated preloads, and those warnings are the point of page 03.

the pages

  • 01-late-discovery.html — the LCP image is a CSS background and the app's data comes from a module. Both are discovered two hops in.
  • 02-preloaded.html — the same page with three preloads. Compare LCP.
  • 03-traps.html — five ways a preload backfires. Read the console.
  • 04-too-many.html — preloading everything, which is the same as prioritising nothing.

the syntax, with the parts people forget

<link rel="preload" as="image" href="/hero.avif" fetchpriority="high">
<link rel="preload" as="font"  href="/inter.woff2" type="font/woff2" crossorigin>
<link rel="preload" as="fetch" href="/api/me" crossorigin>
<link rel="modulepreload"      href="/app.js">   <!-- also preloads its imports -->

<!-- responsive images: preload must match what the img would pick, or you download twice -->
<link rel="preload" as="image" href="/hero-800.avif"
      imagesrcset="/hero-400.avif 400w, /hero-800.avif 800w" imagesizes="100vw">
  • as is not optional. It sets the priority, the Accept header, and the CSP directive checked. Without it the browser fetches with the wrong priority and often can't match the preload to the later request — so it downloads twice.
  • crossorigin is mandatory for as="font" and as="fetch", even same-origin. Both are CORS-mode fetches, and a non-CORS preload cannot be reused by a CORS request.
  • A preload that isn't used within a few seconds produces a console warning and has spent bandwidth for nothing.
